## How this document works

Tier 1 takes someone with no quality background into a first quality role on a midstream construction job: contractor QC technician or owner-side junior inspector, on the right-of-way and inside the fence.

- Each module lists what a graduate can do, as numbered statements that can be tested. Each starts with a verb a test can check: identify, read, record, verify, explain, find, calculate.
- Video scripts, gate exercises and exam questions are written against these statements. Content that serves no statement gets cut. A statement with no question testing it is a gap.
- Tier 1 recognizes and records. It does not accept or reject work that needs a CWI, API 1169, AMPP or NDE certification to sign, and each module says where that line falls.
- Contractor QC and owner inspection do much of the same work under different reporting lines. Statements apply to both unless marked.
- Tier 1 covers gas and hazardous liquid pipelines and their stations (49 CFR 192 and 195, ASME B31.8 and B31.4). Where the rules differ, the statement names both.
- Where conventions vary by project, the statement tests finding the requirement in the project documents, not memorizing one company's answer.
- Out of scope: environmental inspection.

**Statement IDs**

- Each statement's number is its permanent ID. The full ID adds the tier: T1.6.12 is Tier 1, Module 6, statement 12. Every question, video section and gate exercise in the LMS is tagged with the full IDs it tests.
- A new statement takes the next unused number in its module, wherever it sits in the list, so numbers can run out of order.
- A removed statement's ID is retired and never reused.
- A reworded statement keeps its ID only if it still tests the same skill. If the skill changes, or the statement moves to another module, the old ID is retired and a new one issued, so every question tagged to the old ID gets reviewed.

## Module 1: How a job runs and where QC sits

A graduate can name the parties on a midstream job, place a QC role in the reporting structure, and follow the construction sequence on the right-of-way and inside the fence, naming what QC checks and records at each step.

**Who's who**

- 1.1 Name the parties on a midstream job and what each one owns: the operator (owner), the owner's engineer or EPC firm, the prime contractor, specialty subcontractors (HDD and bores, coating, civil, NDE, hydrotest, electrical and instrumentation), third-party inspection firms, and vendors and fabrication shops.
- 1.2 Identify which party employs, pays and directs a contractor QC tech, an owner inspector and a third-party NDE technician working the same job.
- 1.3 Sketch the owner's inspection organization on a construction site: chief inspector, assistant chief inspector, then discipline inspectors (welding, coating, utility and craft).
- 1.4 Sketch the contractor's quality organization (QC manager, QC inspectors and document control) and show where it reports separately from production.
- 1.5 Sketch the same structure inside the fence: construction manager, discipline leads (civil, mechanical, electrical and instrumentation), and QC assigned by discipline.
- 1.6 Explain who a QC finding goes to (the foreman or superintendent for contractor QC, contractor supervision for owner inspection) and why neither role directs craft on how to do the work.

**On the right-of-way**

- 1.7 Put the mainline crews in working order: survey and staking, clearing, grading, ditching, stringing, bending, line-up and welding, NDE, field joint coating, lower-in, backfill, tie-ins, hydrotest, and cleanup and restoration. Explain that HDD, bore and tie-in crews work apart from the main spread, and that ditching can run before or after stringing.
- 1.8 For each mainline crew, name the main QC check and the record it produces: the pipe tally at stringing, the bend log at bending, the weld map and weld log on the firing line, NDE reports, coating and holiday detection records, the lower-in and depth-of-cover check, and the hydrotest package.
- 1.9 Explain why crews on a spread can be miles apart and how that decides which inspector covers which crew.
- 1.10 Read and write a station number (125+50 is 12,550 feet from the start of the line) and explain why every right-of-way record carries one.
- 1.11 Explain what a station equation is (for example, 150+00 back = 148+75 ahead) and why a record near one must state which stationing it uses.

**Inside the fence**

- 1.12 Put the facility sequence in order: civil and foundations, structural steel, equipment setting, spool receiving, field fabrication and field welds, pipe supports, bolt-up, electrical and instrumentation, pressure testing, mechanical completion, commissioning, and turnover. Explain which of these overlap.
- 1.13 Explain why more QC happens inside the fence than on the right-of-way: more joint types per foot (butt, socket, branch, fillet), more components from more vendors, shop and field welds meeting in the same line, flanged and bolted connections, and many disciplines working in a small area.
- 1.14 Explain how a facility is broken into systems, subsystems and test packages, and why every record must roll up to one of them for testing and turnover.
- 1.15 Name the disciplines a QC tech works alongside inside the fence (civil, structural, piping, welding, coating, rotating equipment, electrical and instrumentation). Say which ones Tier 1 covers (piping, welding, materials, NDE, coating, bolting, testing) and which belong to other discipline inspectors.

**Gate exercise:** sort two shuffled sets of activity cards, one from a mainline spread and one from a compressor or pump station, into working order. For each activity, use the sample inspection and test plan to name the record it produces and who signs it: contractor QC, owner inspector, third party, or more than one.

## Module 2: Quality fundamentals

A graduate can explain how quality is controlled on a job, carry a nonconformance through its paperwork, and keep records that survive turnover.

**QA and QC**

- 2.1 Explain the difference between quality assurance (the system: plans, procedures, audits) and quality control (checks on the work itself), with two field examples of each.
- 2.2 Explain why the person checking the work is independent of the person building it, and what that means for who signs which record.
- 2.3 Define hold, witness and review points, and state what the crew may and may not do at each.

**Nonconformances**

- 2.4 Tell a nonconformance apart from an observation, a punch item and a field change. Explain why a weld repair made under the approved repair procedure is not an NCR, and when excessive repairs become one (for example, a repair rate over the project limit, or more repairs on one weld than the spec allows).
- 2.5 Walk an NCR through its life: identify, contain and tag, document, notify, disposition, approve the disposition, implement, verify, close.
- 2.6 Name the four common dispositions (use as-is, repair, rework, reject and replace) and who has authority to approve each. Explain why the field never approves use as-is or repair.
- 2.7 Write an NCR description that states the requirement, the condition found, the location and identifiers, and the evidence, with no opinion or blame.
- 2.8 Recognize when a condition calls for stopping work and calling it in now, not just writing an NCR.

**Root cause and lessons learned**

- 2.9 Tell a correction (fix this one) from a corrective action (stop it happening again) and a preventive action (stop it before it happens anywhere).
- 2.10 Run a basic root cause on a field example with 5 Whys and a fishbone, and separate the root cause from the symptom and from blame.
- 2.11 Write a lessons-learned entry: what happened, why, what changed, who needs to know, and where it is filed so the next job finds it.

**Document control**

- 2.12 Explain what document control is (issuing, revising, distributing and retrieving controlled documents and records) and why it matters: work built to a superseded revision is rework, and a record nobody can find fails an audit.
- 2.13 Read a revision block and confirm the copy in hand is the current controlled revision against the document register before using it.
- 2.14 Explain what to do with a superseded copy found in the field.
- 2.15 Explain transmittals and status codes (issued for construction, for review, approved, approved as noted, rejected), knowing the codes vary by project.
- 2.16 Explain a review cycle: a submittal goes out, comes back with a status and comments, and open comments are tracked to closure. Explain why the audit trail (who reviewed, what changed, and when) has to be kept.
- 2.17 Explain who may redline a drawing in the field, how a redline is controlled, and how redlines become as-builts.
- 2.18 Apply record rules: record from the source at the time, never backfill, correct with a single line, initials and date (or the system's audit trail), and leave no blanks.

**Calibration control**

- 2.19 Explain calibration control: every tool used to measure or accept work (pressure gauges and recorders, torque wrenches, hydraulic pumps and gauges, tensioners, clamp meters, thermometers, film thickness gauges, holiday detectors) carries a unique ID, a current calibration certificate traceable to national standards, and a label showing its due date.
- 2.20 Keep the site calibration log: tool ID, description, serial number, calibration date, due date, certificate number, who holds the tool, and status. Check the log and the label before a tool is used, pull tools before they come due, and add every tool that arrives on site.
- 2.21 Tell a calibration from a field check (such as verifying a holiday detector's voltage or checking a film thickness gauge on shims), and explain that a field check does not replace calibration.
- 2.22 When a tool is found out of calibration, damaged or past due, tag it out, record it, and trace every reading and acceptance made with it since its last good calibration for evaluation, usually through an NCR.

**Turnover**

- 2.23 List what a turnover package holds on a pipeline job and on a facility job: material records, weld records, NDE, coating, test packages, as-builts, closed NCRs.
- 2.24 Explain progressive turnover: packages built and reviewed as each section or system is finished, not at the end, and why it saves rework.
- 2.25 Explain final turnover: punch list, package review, client comments, closure, and handover signatures.
- 2.26 Check a sample package against its index and find a missing or inconsistent record.

**Reviewing completed records**

- 2.27 Explain the difference between a record that is filled out correctly (complete, legible, on the current form, inside the stated limits, signed) and one that is accurate (it tells the truth about the work). Explain that a Tier 1 reviewer can confirm the first and can only look for signs of the second.
- 2.28 Review a completed form, such as a torque form, coating report, weld log or test record, against its requirements: current form revision, every required field filled, units shown, no blank or N/A without a reason, corrections made by the record rules, the right people signing and dating, and every value inside the limits the procedure or spec sets.
- 2.29 Cross-check a form against the records around it: the same weld, joint, heat and tag numbers and dates as the logs and drawings; every instrument on the calibration log and in date on the day it was used; and the technician, welder or applicator qualified on that date.
- 2.30 Recognize signs that a correctly filled record may not be accurate: identical readings across many items, values sitting exactly on the target, one handwriting for several technicians, more work recorded than the crew could do in the time, an instrument used outside its calibration dates, or dates and times out of order with the work.
- 2.31 Spot-check a record against the work where Tier 1 can: the tag, stencil or ID on the item, a witnessed repeat of the activity, or a measurement the graduate is trained to take, such as dry film thickness.
- 2.32 When a record is complete but its accuracy is in doubt, neither correct it nor accept it: document what was found, hold the item if the procedure allows, and refer it to the discipline expert (CWI, coating inspector, bolting lead, NDE Level II) or the QC manager.
- 2.33 Sample records for an audit: pick at random, trace each record forward to the work and back to its source records, and widen the sample when errors turn up.
- 2.34 Record the review itself: what was reviewed, against which requirement and revision, the findings, and the reviewer's name and date, so the review can be audited too.

**Gate exercise:** write an NCR from a field scenario and propose the correction and corrective action. Find the past-due and missing tools on a sample site calibration log. Review a completed torque form and a coating report and find the empty fields, the value outside its limit, the out-of-calibration instrument, and the record that is filled out correctly but shows signs it isn't accurate. Then find the errors planted in a sample turnover package.

## Module 3: The paperwork hierarchy

A graduate can say where any requirement on the job comes from, which document governs when two disagree, and what to read on day one of a new project.

Layers, top down:

1. Regulation: 49 CFR 192 (gas) and 195 (hazardous liquids), plus state pipeline safety rules.
2. Codes and standards the regulation or contract invokes: API 1104, ASME Section IX, ASME B31.8, B31.4 and B31.3.
3. Owner specifications and standards.
4. The contract, project specifications and drawings.
5. Approved procedures: WPS, coating, bolting, testing and NDE.

Alongside every layer: the contractor's QMS.

Read it top down: each layer can tighten the one above it, and the contractor's QMS runs alongside on every job.

**Where requirements come from**

- 3.1 Name the layers that put requirements on a midstream job: regulation (49 CFR 192 for gas, 195 for hazardous liquids, plus state pipeline safety rules), the codes and standards the regulation or contract invokes (API 1104, ASME Section IX, ASME B31.8 and B31.4, and B31.3 at process plants and LNG facilities), the owner's specifications and standards, the contract, project specifications and drawings, approved procedures, and the contractor's own QMS.
- 3.2 Find a regulation in the eCFR (ecfr.gov, free to read) and explain where codes and standards come from: bought from the publisher (API, ASME, AWS) or read through the employer's or project's licensed copies.
- 3.3 Explain that regulation and code set the minimum: the owner can require more, never less. Give examples of owner choices above code: more NDE than code requires, approved-manufacturer lists, extra hold points, tighter acceptance limits.
- 3.4 Identify requirements that come from regulation and that neither the owner nor the contractor can waive on a project, such as qualified welding procedures and welders, required pressure tests, and the records the operator must keep.
- 3.5 Explain that the code edition in force is the one the regulation or contract names, which may not be the latest edition.
- 3.6 Explain that owner specifications are built on regulation and code, so contractor QC and owner inspectors can rely on them, but that a spec usually restates only the requirements it makes stricter or treats as critical. Everything else still applies from the regulation and code, even where the spec is silent.

**The contractor's QMS and the project quality plan**

- 3.7 Explain that the contractor's QMS (quality manual, plus procedures for calibration, document control, NCRs and training records) applies on every job, whoever the client is.
- 3.8 Explain the project quality plan: what it is, who writes it, who approves it, and how it ties the contractor's QMS to this project's requirements. Explain that not every job requires one, smaller jobs especially, and that without one the contract, the specifications and the contractor's QMS still set the requirements.
- 3.9 Explain what happens when the company QMS and a project requirement disagree: the conflict goes to the quality manager in writing and is settled before the work, never in the field.
- 3.10 Read an ITP line: activity, reference document, acceptance criteria, record produced, and the hold, witness and review marks for contractor, owner and third party.
- 3.11 Build a craft-specific ITP for one activity, such as bolted joint torquing: the steps in order, the reference document and acceptance criteria for each, the record each produces, and the hold, witness and review points.
- 3.12 Explain that work proceeds only under approved procedures (WPS, coating, bolting, testing, NDE), and check that the approved revision is the one in use.

**When documents disagree**

- 3.13 Find the order-of-precedence clause in a contract and apply it. Most contracts put drawings over specifications for project-specific details, some reverse it, many say the more stringent of two conflicting requirements governs, and none can go below a code minimum.
- 3.14 When two documents disagree in the field, stop and document the conflict. If the precedence clause settles it, apply it and record which requirement governed. If it does not, or the contract says the stricter requirement governs and which one is stricter is not clear, raise an RFI and get the answer in writing. Never pick one on your own.
- 3.15 Explain RFIs, field change requests and engineering change notices (names vary by project), and why a verbal answer is not a change.
- 3.16 Explain how a requirement is formally deviated (a deviation, variance or concession request), who approves it, and that a regulatory requirement cannot be waived this way on a project.

**Day one on a project**

- 3.17 On starting a project, find and read: the PQP (if the job has one), the ITPs for your scope, the specifications that touch your work, the governing code editions, your company's QMS procedures for your role, the NCR procedure, the document register, and the list of who may sign what.
- 3.18 Given a field question (what preheat applies, what holiday test voltage, is this bolt grade allowed), trace it to the governing document and cite the section.

**Gate exercise:** a requirements trace. From a packet of excerpts (contract precedence clause, owner spec, project spec, drawing note, WPS, code section), answer six field questions with the governing document and section. One question hides a conflict the student must raise as an RFI.

## Module 4: Reading drawings

A graduate can find the right drawing for a piece of work, read what it requires, and check the built work against it, on the right-of-way and inside the fence.

**The drawing set**

- 4.1 Name the drawing types on a pipeline job (alignment sheets, crossing drawings, HDD plan and profiles, tie-in drawings, standard details) and on a facility job (P&IDs, plot plans, piping plans and sections, isometrics, spool drawings, pipe support details, vendor drawings), and say what question each one answers.
- 4.2 Read a title block: drawing number, sheet, title, revision, status, scale and approval signatures. Identify which drawings may be built from (issued for construction) and which may not.
- 4.3 Match a revision cloud and its triangle to the revision block and say what changed. Find a hold cloud or hold note and explain that the held area is not built until the hold is lifted.
- 4.4 Follow references between drawings: match lines, continuation sheets, detail and section callouts, reference drawing lists and standard detail numbers.
- 4.5 Read the general notes and drawing notes, and explain that a note carries the same weight as the picture.

**Pipeline drawings**

- 4.6 Read an alignment sheet: plan view, profile, stationing and the data band. Given a station, find the wall thickness, grade, coating, depth of cover and any crossing.
- 4.7 Explain why wall thickness, coating or weighting changes along the line (class location on gas lines, road and rail crossings, bores and HDDs, water crossings) and find where each change starts and ends by station.
- 4.8 Read a crossing drawing and an HDD plan and profile: entry and exit points, depth, required cover or separation from the foreign line, and the pipe specified for the crossing.

**Facility drawings**

- 4.9 Read a P&ID: equipment, line numbers, valves, instruments, flow direction and spec breaks. Explain that a P&ID shows how the system connects, not where the pipe runs or how long it is.
- 4.10 Decode a line number with the project's line designation legend (for example size, service, sequence number, piping class and insulation) and find that line on the P&ID, the piping plan and its isometrics.
- 4.11 Use a piping class sheet to find the pipe, fittings, flanges, gaskets, bolting and valves allowed for a line by size and rating.
- 4.12 Read an isometric: flow and north arrows, dimensions, elevations, coordinates, the bill of materials, shop and field weld marks, spool breaks, supports and continuation to other sheets. Explain that an isometric is not drawn to scale.
- 4.13 Read a spool drawing and match the spool number and piece marks to the isometric and its bill of materials.
- 4.14 Read a pipe support detail and find the support type and location for a line.
- 4.15 Read a vendor drawing for nozzle size, rating and orientation. Recognize civil, structural and electrical and instrumentation drawings as belonging to other discipline inspectors.

**Reading the details**

- 4.16 Read plan, elevation, section and detail views, and say which direction each one looks.
- 4.17 Read dimensions and elevations in feet and inches, decimal feet and millimeters, including centerline, bottom of pipe and top of steel callouts. Convert between feet and inches and decimal feet.
- 4.18 Locate a point by plant coordinates (north and east), and explain why a plot plan uses plant north, which can differ from true north.
- 4.19 Explain why dimensions are read and never scaled off a drawing, and raise an RFI when a dimension the work needs is missing.
- 4.20 Identify common piping symbols: valve types, flanges, reducers, tees, caps, blinds and spectacle blinds, instruments and weld points.
- 4.21 Read a basic weld symbol: reference line, arrow side and other side, fillet and groove, size, weld all-around and the field weld flag.

**From drawing to field**

- 4.22 Verify installed pipe against its isometric: line number, routing and orientation, dimensions within the project tolerance, components and materials per the bill of materials, and weld locations.
- 4.23 Recognize field verify (FV) dimensions and field fit welds (FFW) on a drawing. Explain that the piping crew measures and sets these in the field, usually where new pipe meets existing pipe or equipment, and that a field fit spool ships with extra length to be cut to fit. Check that the measured dimension reaches the fabricator before an off-site spool is built, and goes on the redline so it reaches the as-built.
- 4.24 Record a difference between the drawing and the field with the drawing number, revision, location and what was found, and route it as a redline or RFI.

**Gate exercise:** a drawing packet with one alignment sheet and one facility set (P&ID, piping plan, two isometrics, piping class sheet). Find the wall thickness, coating and cover at a given station. Decode a line number and find it on every drawing. Check three bill of materials items against the class sheet, one of which is wrong. Mark the field welds on an isometric. Find the sheet that is not the current revision or that sits under a hold.

## Module 5: Material and traceability

A graduate can receive material, read its markings and MTR, match it to the drawing and class sheet, and keep the trail from mill to weld unbroken.

**Why traceability matters**

- 5.1 Explain traceability: every pressure-containing component in the finished line can be traced from its location back to its MTR. Explain that operators of steel gas transmission lines installed after July 1, 2020 must keep pipe material records for the life of the pipeline (49 CFR 192.67), and that owner specs on both gas and liquid lines commonly require the same traceability for pipe, fittings, flanges and valves.
- 5.2 Sort material into what needs an MTR on every regulated job (pressure-containing items: pipe, fittings, flanges, valves) and what the project decides (bolting, gaskets, supports, structural steel), and find the project's rule in the spec or ITP.
- 5.3 Explain what happens to material with no MTR, an MTR that doesn't match, or markings that can't be read: it is tagged, held in quarantine and not installed until dispositioned.

**Markings and identifiers**

- 5.4 Explain the difference between a heat number (one melt of steel, shared by many pieces) and a pipe or joint number (one individual piece), and why the MTR is issued by heat.
- 5.5 Read line pipe markings: manufacturer, specification (API 5L), grade and product specification level, outside diameter, wall thickness, pipe type, heat number, pipe number and length. Name the common pipe types (seamless, ERW or high-frequency welded, longitudinal submerged arc welded or DSAW, and helical or spiral welded) and where each is typically used.
- 5.6 Read fitting, flange and valve markings: manufacturer, material specification and grade, size, pressure class, schedule or wall, face type and heat code, or the valve's tag plate and serial number.
- 5.7 Transfer heat and pipe numbers to each piece before pipe is cut, using the project's suffix convention, so no cut piece loses its identity.

**Grades and strength**

- 5.8 Explain what a grade means: API 5L X52 has a minimum yield strength of 52,000 psi and X70 has 70,000 psi. Explain yield strength (where steel starts to stretch permanently) and tensile strength (the most load it carries before it breaks).
- 5.9 Explain how PSL2 pipe differs from PSL1: tighter chemistry, a maximum as well as a minimum yield, and required toughness (Charpy) testing.
- 5.10 Name the common material specs on a midstream job and match them by strength to the pipe: line pipe (API 5L), high-yield fittings (MSS SP-75 WPHY grades), forged flanges (ASTM A105, and A694 F grades for high-yield lines), and carbon steel fittings and pipe on facility piping (ASTM A234 WPB, A106 B, A53 B). For example, X65 pipe takes WPHY 65 fittings and F65 flanges.
- 5.11 Recognize a wall thickness or strength mismatch between a fitting and the pipe, and find the transition detail the drawing or spec requires.

**MTRs**

- 5.12 Read an MTR: issuer, specification and edition, heat number, items or joints covered, chemistry and carbon equivalent, yield, tensile and elongation, Charpy results, hydrotest, heat treatment and the certification statement. Recognize an EN 10204 type 3.1 certificate.
- 5.13 Check an MTR against the purchase and project specification: each value within the required minimum and maximum, and the heat on the MTR matching the heat on the item.
- 5.14 Recognize signs of a bad record: altered or illegible values, a heat on an item with no MTR, an MTR with no matching heat on site, or markings that don't match the paperwork.

**Receiving and tracking**

- 5.15 Explain a preservation plan: what it requires for each type of material from receipt to installation, who owns it, how often stored material is checked, and how those checks are recorded.
- 5.16 Perform a receiving inspection: count against the packing list and purchase order, check markings against the MTR, look for damage (dents, gouges, bevel and coating damage, missing end caps), and record it on the receiving report.
- 5.17 Match a received item to the drawing: the bill of materials item, the class sheet, and the item's size, rating, schedule or wall, material spec and grade, and face type.
- 5.18 Build and check a pipe tally: pipe number, heat, length, wall, grade, coating and station for each joint, with tallied length reconciled against the stationing.
- 5.19 Store and stack material without damaging it: pipe on padded skids or sand berms, chocked against rolling and within the stack height the spec or manufacturer allows; segregated by grade and wall; end caps and bevel and flange face protectors kept on; coated pipe protected from long UV exposure where the spec limits it; stainless kept off carbon steel; valves, gaskets and coating materials stored per the manufacturer; and markings kept readable.
- 5.20 Explain when PMI (positive material identification) is required (alloy and stainless on facilities, per the project) and record a PMI result against the heat number.

**Gate exercise:** a receiving lot with marking photos for pipe joints, fittings and flanges, their MTRs, a packing list and an isometric bill of materials. Match each item to its MTR and bill of materials line. Find the item whose heat has no MTR, the flange whose class doesn't match the class sheet, and the MTR whose yield is below spec. Then build a tally for six joints, including one cut joint marked correctly.

## Module 6: Weld mapping and weld records

A graduate can number and map every weld on a pipeline or facility job, tie each weld to its material, welders, procedure and NDE, and keep the records complete enough to pass turnover.

**Where Tier 1 stops:** the graduate records NDE results from the NDE report. Interpreting film or ultrasonic data belongs to the certified NDE technician, and visual acceptance of a weld belongs to the CWI or owner welding inspector where the project requires one.

**What a weld map is**

- 6.1 Explain a weld map: a record of every weld's number and location, tied to the material on each side, the welders, the WPS, the date and the NDE result.
- 6.2 Name the records that connect to the weld map (weld log, NDE log and reports, repair log, welder qualification and continuity log, WPS register, pipe tally or material log) and the question each one answers.
- 6.3 Explain what happens when a weld can't be traced to its material, welder or NDE, and why that can cost a cutout.

**Numbering**

- 6.4 Apply the project's mainline numbering convention: sequential or station-based weld numbers, with tie-ins and fabricated sections numbered so they slot into the line.
- 6.5 Apply the facility convention: welds numbered by line, isometric or spool, with shop welds, field welds and field fit welds told apart.
- 6.6 Number repairs, cutouts and replacement welds by the project's suffix rules so each location's history stays visible and no number is reused.

**On the right-of-way**

- 6.7 Record each mainline weld: weld number, station, pipe and heat numbers on both sides, wall and grade, the welder on each pass and their ID mark, the WPS and the date.
- 6.8 Map tie-ins, crossings, HDD pull sections and fabricated assemblies so they join the mainline record at the right station, and flag any weld the project treats as a golden weld.
- 6.9 Reconcile welds against the tally: on one continuous string, welds equal joints minus one, and tie-ins, cutouts and pups change the count. Find the cause when the numbers don't agree.

**Inside the fence**

- 6.10 Mark weld numbers on the isometric and on the pipe, and match shop welds to the fabricator's weld records and field welds to the field log.
- 6.11 Record each facility weld: line and isometric, weld number, joint type (butt, socket, branch, fillet), size and schedule, piece marks or heat numbers on each side, welder IDs, WPS, date and NDE selection.
- 6.12 Explain percentage (random) NDE selection and progressive sampling when a selected weld fails, and track both on the weld map.

**NDE, repairs and cutouts**

- 6.13 Post NDE results to the map (accept, repair or cut out) with the NDE report number, and confirm every weld that requires NDE has a result.
- 6.14 Track a repair: the defect, repair WPS, repair welder, re-examination result, and whether the location is within the number of repairs the spec allows.
- 6.15 Track a cutout: the welds removed, the replacement welds, and the tally update for pipe removed and new pups with their heat numbers.
- 6.16 Check that each welder was qualified for the WPS and in continuity on the date of each weld, using the welder qualification log.

**Keeping the record clean**

- 6.17 Keep the map and logs current daily and from the source, on paper or in weld tracking software, and reconcile the weld log, NDE log and repair log before gaps age.
- 6.18 Audit a weld log: find a weld with no NDE, a welder not qualified on the date welded, a heat with no MTR, a duplicate weld number and a repair over the limit.
- 6.19 Explain how weld records are indexed for turnover (by spread and station, or by system, line and isometric) and what the turnover package needs from them. For liquid lines, 49 CFR 195.266 requires the operator to keep, for the life of the facility, the total number of girth welds, the number examined by NDE, the number rejected and the disposition of each reject.

**Gate exercise:** from a day of field data (welder ID photos, a pipe tally, NDE reports), build the weld map for a mainline section and one facility isometric. Then audit a planted weld log and find every error.

## Module 7: Welding compliance for a non-CWI QC

A graduate can read a WPS and a welder qualification, check that the welding in progress matches both, and record what they measured, without accepting or rejecting the finished weld.

**Where Tier 1 stops:** the graduate checks that welding follows the approved WPS and is done by a qualified welder, and records the readings. Visual acceptance of the finished weld, and qualifying procedures and welders, belong to the CWI or owner welding inspector.

**Procedures and qualifications**

- 7.1 Explain the difference between a WPS (the instructions for making the weld), a PQR (the test record that proves the WPS produces a sound weld) and a welder qualification record (the test record that proves the welder can make it).
- 7.2 Name the code that governs welding qualification on each kind of work: API 1104 for pipeline welding, ASME Section IX for most facility and station piping, and AWS D1.1 for structural steel, with the project spec deciding where more than one could apply.
- 7.3 Explain essential variables: a change outside a qualified range (such as process, material group, wall or diameter range, position, direction of travel, filler metal or preheat) needs a different qualified procedure or welder.

**Reading a WPS**

- 7.4 Read a WPS: process, base material and thickness range, diameter range, joint design, position and direction of travel, filler metal by pass, shielding gas, current type and polarity, amperage and voltage ranges, travel speed, preheat and interpass temperature, time between passes, cleaning, and post-weld heat treatment.
- 7.5 Check that the joint is inside the WPS: material grade, wall thickness, diameter and joint type within the qualified ranges, and the WPS approved and current on the register.

**Welder qualifications**

- 7.6 Read a welder qualification record: welder ID, process, position, diameter and thickness range, material, filler metal, test date and result, and check that it covers the weld being made.
- 7.7 Explain continuity: a welder who hasn't used a process for six months loses the qualification for it under ASME Section IX. Pipeline rules add to this: on liquid lines, 49 CFR 195.222 requires use of the process and one weld tested and found acceptable under API 1104 within the preceding six months, and on gas lines, 49 CFR 192.229 requires recent use of the process and, on higher-stress lines, a recent tested weld. Check continuity records against the date of each weld.
- 7.8 Match the welder ID marked at the joint to a welder qualified for that pass, and recognize someone welding without a qualification on file.

**Checking parameters**

- 7.9 Check fit-up before welding against the WPS and spec: bevel angle, root face, root opening, hi-lo, and when the line-up clamp may come off.
- 7.10 Check filler metal: classification and diameter for each pass match the WPS, and low-hydrogen electrodes come from a rod oven at the required temperature within the allowed exposure time.
- 7.11 Check shielding gas type and flow rate, and wind protection where the process needs it.
- 7.12 Measure amperage, voltage and polarity with a calibrated meter and compare them against the WPS range for that pass.
- 7.13 Calculate travel speed (length welded divided by time) and, where the WPS requires it, heat input (volts × amps × 60 ÷ travel speed in inches per minute, giving joules per inch).
- 7.14 Check preheat and interpass temperature at the distance from the joint the WPS or spec requires, using the temperature crayon rated for the minimum preheat and the one rated for the maximum interpass, or a calibrated contact thermometer.
- 7.15 Check the time between the root and the second pass, cleaning between passes, and the number of welders per pass against the WPS.
- 7.16 Review a post-weld heat treatment chart against the procedure: temperature, hold time, heating and cooling rates, and thermocouple locations.

**Records**

- 7.17 Confirm each meter, thermometer and gauge used is calibrated and in date, and record its ID with the reading.
- 7.18 Record parameter checks on the project form (weld number, pass, welder, readings and WPS ranges), and report any reading outside the range per the project procedure, since the affected weld may need evaluation or cutout.

**Gate exercise:** given a WPS, three welder qualification records and field readings for five welds (amps, volts, travel time, preheat, electrode, welder ID, date), identify which welds were made inside the WPS by a qualified welder, calculate travel speed and heat input, and write the finding for each weld that wasn't.

## Module 8: NDE basics

A graduate can name the NDE methods used on midstream jobs, read an NDE report, check a technician's certification, and keep NDE tied to the weld map.

**Where Tier 1 stops:** the graduate reads results and tracks coverage. Performing NDE and interpreting results belong to a technician certified under the employer's written practice, at Level II or above for interpretation.

**Methods**

- 8.1 Name the methods and what each finds: visual (VT), radiography (RT), ultrasonic (UT, including automated and phased array), magnetic particle (MT) and liquid penetrant (PT). Sort them into surface methods and volumetric methods.
- 8.2 Explain where each method is commonly used: RT or automated UT on mainline girth welds, RT or UT on facility butt welds, MT or PT on fillet, socket and branch welds, and PT on stainless and other non-magnetic material.
- 8.3 Explain when a spec calls for hardness testing (such as after PWHT or for sour service) and record the result against the weld.
- 8.4 Follow radiation safety around RT: stay outside the barricade, obey the signs and lights, and know that the RT crew owns the boundary and its survey readings.

**Certifications and procedures**

- 8.5 Explain technician Levels I, II and III under the employer's written practice (ASNT SNT-TC-1A or ANSI/ASNT CP-189), what each level may do, and check that a technician's certification covers the method being used.
- 8.6 Explain that NDE follows an approved written procedure, and that acceptance criteria come from the governing code or project spec (API 1104 for pipelines, ASME B31.3 for process piping), not the technician's judgment.
- 8.7 Find the required extent of examination (100 percent or a percentage, by weld type and location) in the regulation, code or owner spec, knowing the owner often requires more than code.

**Reading the report**

- 8.8 Read an NDE report: report number, date, procedure, technician and level, welds examined, technique, film or scan ID, indications found, accept or reject for each weld, and signature.
- 8.9 Explain common indications in plain terms (porosity, slag inclusion, lack of fusion, incomplete penetration, burn-through, undercut, cracks) without judging their acceptance.
- 8.10 Reconcile NDE reports with the weld map: every weld needing NDE has a report, weld numbers and stations match, and the wall and material on the report match the joint.
- 8.11 Confirm a repaired or replacement weld was re-examined as the spec requires and the result posted.

**Timing and records**

- 8.12 Apply NDE timing rules: a required delay after welding before examination where the spec sets one, examination after PWHT where required, and no field joint coating until NDE has accepted the weld.
- 8.13 Explain who keeps the radiographs or digital NDE data, for how long, and how they go into turnover.

**Gate exercise:** a set of NDE reports and a weld map. Find the weld with no NDE, the report signed by a technician whose certification doesn't cover the method, the rejected weld never re-examined, and the weld number on a report that doesn't exist on the map.

## Module 9: Coating and paint

A graduate can confirm that the approved coating or paint system from the approved manufacturer is applied per the manufacturer's instructions and the spec, check and record the conditions and results, and find coating damage before the pipe is buried or put in service.

**Where Tier 1 stops:** the graduate checks and records against the spec and the product data. This is not AMPP coating inspector training, and where the project requires a certified coating inspector to accept the work, acceptance is theirs.

**Systems on the job**

- 9.1 Explain that coating and cathodic protection work together to keep buried steel from corroding, and why a single holiday concentrates corrosion at that spot.
- 9.2 Name the coating systems on a midstream job: mill-applied line pipe coating (FBE, FBE with an abrasion-resistant overcoat for bores and HDDs, three-layer polyethylene or polypropylene), field joint coatings (liquid epoxy, FBE, heat-shrink sleeves), concrete coating and weights for buoyancy, transition coatings at soil-to-air interfaces, and above-ground paint systems (primer, intermediate and topcoat).

**Approved systems and product data**

- 9.3 Find the approved coating systems in the spec or approved-products list, and confirm the product in use matches by manufacturer, product name and number, and color where specified.
- 9.4 Find the manufacturer's product data sheet and application guide (on the manufacturer's website or in the approved submittal) and read the limits: surface prep, air and steel temperature, dew point margin, humidity, mix ratio, induction time, pot life, film thickness range, recoat window and cure time. Apply the spec where it is stricter.
- 9.5 Record batch numbers and expiry dates from the containers, reject expired material, and check storage against the manufacturer's conditions.
- 9.6 Check that the applicator and procedure are qualified where the spec requires it.
- 9.7 Read the paint schedule for a facility and find the system required for each area and service, including insulated and high-temperature lines.

**Conditions and surface prep**

- 9.8 Measure and record air temperature, relative humidity, steel temperature and dew point with calibrated instruments, and confirm the steel is at least 5°F (3°C) above the dew point or the margin the spec requires.
- 9.9 Check surface prep against the cleanliness standard the spec names (such as near-white blast, SSPC-SP 10 / NACE No. 2), measure anchor profile with replica tape or a profile gauge, and check for dust, oil and salts where the spec requires testing.

**Application and inspection**

- 9.10 Check mix ratio, induction time and pot life during mixing, and wet film thickness during application.
- 9.11 Measure dry film thickness with a calibrated gauge by the method the spec names (such as SSPC-PA 2 for paint) and record the readings against the required range.
- 9.12 Confirm cure before handling, recoating or backfill, by time and temperature from the data sheet or by the test the spec names.
- 9.13 Run holiday detection: set the voltage from the spec, verify the detector on site, cover the full surface at the right speed, and mark and record each holiday for repair.
- 9.14 Check heat-shrink sleeves: preheat, overlap onto the mill coating, no wrinkles or voids, and adhesive showing at the edges, per the manufacturer.
- 9.15 Check coating repairs: the approved repair product for the size of the damage, prep, application and a holiday retest.
- 9.16 Check coated pipe through handling and lower-in: padded skids, slings and supports, a final holiday test before lower-in, and padding or rock shield where required.

**Records**

- 9.17 Write a coating report: location or joint numbers, product and batch, conditions, prep results, film thickness, holidays and repairs, and instrument IDs.

**Gate exercise:** from a spec excerpt, a product data sheet and a day of field joint coating readings, find the joint coated inside the dew point margin, the batch past its expiry, the film thickness below range and the product that isn't on the approved list. Set the holiday detector voltage from the spec.

## Module 10: Bolted connections

A graduate can check that a flanged joint is assembled from the right parts, tightened by the approved method and pattern with calibrated tools, and recorded so every joint can be traced and signed off.

**Where Tier 1 stops:** the graduate checks and records. Setting the bolt load belongs to engineering, and qualifying bolting technicians belongs to the bolting contractor where the project requires it.

**Parts**

- 10.1 Name the parts of a flanged joint and confirm each matches the class sheet and drawing: flange type (weld neck, slip-on, blind, orifice), class and face (raised face or ring type joint), gasket, studs and nuts (such as ASTM A193 B7 studs with A194 2H nuts, or the coated and low-temperature grades the spec calls for), stud diameter and length.
- 10.2 Check gaskets: the right type, size, class and material (spiral wound with the required inner and outer rings, ring joint by ring number, sheet), new and never reused, and stored flat and clean.
- 10.3 Check flange faces before assembly: the correct face type and finish, no scratches or damage across the sealing surface, and clean undamaged ring grooves on ring type joints.
- 10.4 Check that studs are long enough to protrude past each nut by the amount the spec requires.
- 10.5 Check that the approved lubricant is applied to threads and nut faces, and explain why a different lubricant changes the bolt load a given torque produces.

**Alignment and assembly**

- 10.6 Check alignment before bolting: flange faces parallel, centerlines in line, bolt holes lined up and studs going in by hand, within the tolerance the spec or ASME PCC-1 sets. Explain why a joint is never pulled into alignment with its bolts.

**Tightening methods**

- 10.7 Explain manual torque, hydraulic torque and hydraulic tensioning: how each loads the bolt, and when each is used.
- 10.8 Follow a torque procedure: the target torque for that bolt size and lubricant, the passes at increasing percentages of target, the pattern (cross or star pattern, or another pattern ASME PCC-1 allows), and the final passes at full torque until the nuts stop turning.
- 10.9 Convert a target torque to hydraulic pump pressure using the chart for that specific tool by serial number, and check the reaction arm is placed safely.
- 10.10 Follow a tensioning procedure: tool coverage (all studs or half at a time), applied pressure from the procedure including the allowance for load lost when the tool is removed, the passes, and nut rundown checks.

**Calibration**

- 10.11 Confirm torque wrenches, hydraulic pumps, gauges and tensioners are calibrated and in date, and matched by serial number to their certificate and pressure chart. Record the tool IDs.
- 10.12 When a tool is found out of calibration, tag it out and trace every joint tightened with it since its last good calibration.

**Records**

- 10.13 Give each joint a unique number on the drawing and a tag on the flange, and record: joint number, flange size and class, gasket, bolting, lubricant, method, target torque or pressure, tool IDs, technician, and sign-off.
- 10.14 Track temporary gaskets and test blinds through pressure testing so each is replaced with the permanent gasket and recorded before turnover.
- 10.15 Walk down completed joints for the ITP sign-off: every nut tight, stud protrusion, correct tags, and the joint register complete.

**Gate exercise:** given a joint list, a bolting procedure, a tool calibration chart and a completed torque log, convert the target torque to pump pressure, then find the joint with the wrong gasket, the joint tightened out of pattern, the out-of-calibration tool and the missing final pass.

## Module 11: Tie-ins, fabrication and pressure testing

A graduate can follow a tie-in from plan to record, check fabricated piping from the shop to its final position, and carry a pressure test from test package to signed record, knowing who approves each step.

**Tie-ins**

- 11.1 Explain what a tie-in is on the right-of-way (joining sections, crossings and HDD pulls) and inside the fence (new piping to existing piping or equipment), and why tie-in welds get extra attention: they are often made in bell holes or tight spaces, under fit-up stress, and some can't be pressure tested with the line.
- 11.2 Check a tie-in: fit-up made without forcing the pipe, the WPS and welders qualified for the position, pups marked with transferred heat and pipe numbers, and the weld mapped to both sections.
- 11.3 Explain a golden weld: a weld that can't be pressure tested with the line it joins, so the spec requires other verification instead, usually 100 percent radiographic or ultrasonic examination and documented owner approval.
- 11.4 Identify golden welds before they are made, confirm the approval and the extra NDE are in the ITP, and flag them on the weld map and in the test package.
- 11.5 Explain pretested pipe and assemblies (tested before installation so the tie-in welds are the only untested welds) and trace each pretested piece to its test record.
- 11.6 Recognize hot taps and line stops as specialty work done under their own procedures and qualified crews.

**Fabrication**

- 11.7 Explain what is built in a fabrication shop (spools, headers, skids, launchers and receivers) and what is built in the field, and the shop records that come with each piece in the data book (MTRs, weld map, NDE, PWHT, dimensional report, coating).
- 11.8 Explain source inspection: the owner or third party inspects at the shop and issues a release, and the release is checked at receiving.
- 11.9 Review a data book on receipt: spool numbers match the isometrics, and weld records, NDE, material traceability, dimensions, coating and PWHT are complete before the spool is accepted.
- 11.10 Check a spool on receipt: dimensions and orientation against the isometric, flange bolt-hole orientation, bevel and flange face protection, internal cleanliness, coating damage and markings.
- 11.11 Check field fabrication and installation: dimensions, supports installed per the support details (shoes, guides, anchors and gaps), and no welded attachments to piping that the drawings don't show.

**Pressure testing: what and who**

- 11.12 Explain why a pressure test is done (strength and leak tightness), the test types (hydrostatic, pneumatic and in-service leak test), and why hydrostatic testing is preferred: compressed gas stores far more energy than water, so a pneumatic failure is far more dangerous.
- 11.13 Find the test requirements (medium, test pressure, hold time and acceptance) in the regulation (49 CFR 192 Subpart J for gas, 49 CFR 195 Subpart E for liquids), the code (ASME B31.8, B31.4, B31.3) and the approved test procedure.
- 11.14 Explain who approves what: the test procedure is approved before testing, the test package is reviewed and released by contractor QC and the owner before filling, the test is witnessed per the ITP, and the owner accepts the result. The testing contractor runs the test but does not accept it.

**Pressure testing: before the test**

- 11.15 Build and review a test package: the test boundary marked on P&IDs or isometrics, test limits (blinds, temporary closures, valve positions), weld map and NDE complete for every weld inside the boundary, golden welds identified, punch items that must close before the test, instrument list, test pressure calculation and the approved procedure.
- 11.16 Calculate test pressure at the high and low points from elevation (water adds about 0.433 psi per foot of head) and confirm the high point reaches the minimum and the low point stays under the maximum.
- 11.17 Check test instruments: gauges, recorders and the deadweight tester calibrated and in date, gauge range within what the spec allows, and temperature recorders on the pipe and ground where the procedure requires them.

**Pressure testing: during and after**

- 11.18 Witness a test: fill and air removal, staged pressurization, stabilization, the hold period with pressure and temperature logged at set intervals, chart start and end signed, and leak examination only at the pressure the procedure allows.
- 11.19 Follow test safety: exclusion zones, nobody near the boundary during pressurization, and the extra controls a pneumatic test requires.
- 11.20 Explain a failed test: depressurize, find the leak, repair or cut out under an NCR, and retest the affected section. Nothing is repaired under pressure.
- 11.21 Follow reinstatement after the test: depressurize, drain, dry where required, remove test blinds and temporary gaskets, install and record permanent gaskets, and track the joints broken after the test to their own tightness check.
- 11.22 Complete the test record the operator keeps while the line is in service. For liquid lines, 49 CFR 195.310 requires the pressure charts, test instrument calibration data, the operator and test company names and the person responsible, date and time, minimum test pressure, test medium, a description of the facility and test apparatus, an explanation of chart irregularities and failures, an elevation profile where elevation varies more than 100 feet, and the test medium or pipe temperature. For gas lines tested under 49 CFR 192.505 or 192.507, 192.517 requires the operator and test company names and the person responsible, test medium, pressure, duration, the charts or readings, elevation differences where they matter, and leaks or failures and their disposition.

**Gate exercise:** a test package with an elevation profile and planted problems: a weld in the boundary with no NDE, an unflagged golden weld, an out-of-date gauge and a test pressure that is too high at the low point. Calculate the high and low point pressures, find each problem, and name who must approve the package before filling and who accepts the test.

## Module 12: Conduct on site

A graduate can work a QC role day to day on either side of the contract: knowing what their role owns, reporting what they see, keeping themselves safe, and keeping their signature worth something.

**Two roles, one job**

- 12.1 Compare contractor QC and owner inspection: the contractor QC tech builds the record and answers for the contractor's quality, and the owner inspector verifies the work and the record and accepts for the operator. Name three tasks both roles do and two that only one does.
- 12.2 Explain what to do when contractor QC and the owner inspector disagree on a result: state the requirement, document both positions, and take it up your own reporting line, not on the crew.
- 12.3 Explain who can change your priorities (your supervisor) and who can't (a craft foreman), and how to handle a client or contractor request outside your scope.

**The day**

- 12.4 Run a day: the safety meeting and JSA, the plan for the crews you cover, the hold and witness points due, inspections and records through the shift, and the daily report at the end.
- 12.5 Write a daily report: date, weather, crews and locations by station or system, work observed, inspections and results, hold points released, issues and NCRs raised, instruments used, and open items for tomorrow, in facts with no opinion.
- 12.6 Keep field notes or an electronic log from the source as the work happens, knowing they are records that can be requested in an audit or a dispute.

**Safety**

- 12.7 Follow site safety as a QC tech: PPE, the JSA, permits (hot work, confined space, excavation), staying out of equipment swing radius and the line of fire, and never entering a trench 5 feet or deeper without a protective system, as OSHA requires.
- 12.8 Explain stop-work authority: anyone can stop work for safety, quality holds follow the quality plan, how to call a stop, and what gets documented after.

**Integrity**

- 12.9 Explain what a QC signature means: you saw it. Never sign for work you didn't witness, never backdate, and never record a reading you didn't take. Explain that falsified records can end a career and, on regulated pipelines, lead to federal penalties.
- 12.10 Recognize pressure to pass work (schedule, a friendly foreman, "we've always done it this way"), state the requirement, document it and escalate.
- 12.11 Explain why inspectors don't accept gifts from the contractors and vendors they inspect, and disclose relationships that could look like a conflict of interest.

**Communication**

- 12.12 Report a finding to the right person, specifically and factually, with the requirement cited, and follow it into the record.
- 12.13 Own a mistake in the record: correct it by the record rules, tell your supervisor, and don't hide it.

**Gate exercise:** a shift scenario with events in order: a crew passes a hold point, a foreman asks you to sign the log for welds you didn't see, a trench has no protection, and a client rep asks for a check outside your scope. Say what you did at each event and who you told, then write the daily report.
